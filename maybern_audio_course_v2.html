How This Course Was Written

What "Optimized for Audio Learning" Actually Means

Most learning materials are written to be read. When you write to be read, you reach for tools that make things scannable — bullet points, headers, tables, bold text, diagrams. Those devices do real work when a reader can jump around, re-read a sentence, or scan ahead. But when you're running on a treadmill, they become invisible. There are no bullet points in a podcast. No headers in a lecture. And if you close your eyes and listen to someone read a table of data, you're going to retain almost none of it.

That's the problem this course was designed to solve. Every chapter was written with a specific constraint: if you read it aloud, it should sound like a knowledgeable human explaining something to you — not a document being recited. That constraint changes the writing in four specific ways.

The first is building the mental image before naming the thing. In most documentation, you introduce a concept by naming it and then defining it. "A capital call is a request from the GP to LPs for a portion of their committed capital." Fine if you're reading. But for a listener, you've asked them to hold an unfamiliar term in working memory for half a second before they know what it means. Multiply that across hundreds of terms and you get a listener who's constantly playing catch-up.

The audio approach inverts this. You build the picture first — what's happening, what the workflow looks like, what it feels like — and let the name arrive after the listener already understands the concept. By the time they hear "capital call," they're nodding, not scrambling.

The second is elaborative encoding. When you hear a concept in isolation, it enters memory like a loose thread. When you hear it woven into an analogy, a story, or a concrete example, it gets anchored to something you already know. The brain stores information in webs of association, not in files. Every analogy in this course is an attempt to attach a new concept to a web that already exists in your mind. That's not a simplification. It's a hook. And the hook is what makes the concept retrievable during a client meeting three weeks from now.

The third is explicit signposting — the audio equivalent of visual hierarchy. On a page, size and weight and spacing show relationships. A heading is bigger. A sub-section is indented. In audio, you do all of that with words. "Here's the first thing." "The second thing builds on that." "Now here's the exception." "Let me tie this back to what we said in the last chapter." These transitions feel almost redundant when you read them. When you hear them, they're navigation. They tell you where you are in the logic and give your brain permission to close one idea before opening the next.

The fourth is spaced reinforcement. A concept introduced in Chapter 3 shows up again in Chapter 7 in a different context. Not because it was repeated carelessly — but because research on the spacing effect says information is retained better when you encounter it multiple times over time rather than once in depth. Each time you hear a term in a new context, you're building another path into the concept in memory.

One more thing worth noting. This course is not trying to replace documentation. It's not the definitive reference you consult when you need the exact mechanics of a European waterfall. For that — you want a spec sheet or the Maybern docs. What this course is trying to give you is fluency. The ability to discuss these concepts without looking them up. To recognize a situation in a client meeting and know what domain it lives in. To ask the right follow-up question because you understand the shape of the problem.

Fluency comes from immersion — repeated casual exposure to the vocabulary and logic of a domain. And the best time to build fluency happens to be the same time most people are looking for something to listen to. On a run. In a car. On a walk.

This course is designed to work alongside the Fund Management 101 Textbook and Glossary in Notion. The course gives you fluency. The textbook and glossary give you precision. Use both.

So that's the design. No bullet points — because bullet points are silent. No tables — because tables are invisible. No visual callouts — because your eyes are busy. Just voice, structure, and story. The three things that actually work when learning happens with your ears.

Part 1: The Industry Chapter 1

What Is a Closed-End Private Fund

Structure, Lifecycle, LP/GP Relationship, How Money Flows

Welcome to this course. I'm going to walk you through the operational and accounting reality of how alternative investment funds work. Not the textbook version — the version that matters when you're sitting across from a fund controller who manages fourteen funds and forty LPs and hasn't slept well since the last quarterly close.

If you're listening to this because you work at Maybern, or you're in fund administration, fund accounting, or investor relations at a PE, VC, or infrastructure fund — this course was designed for you. By the end of Part One, you'll understand why fund accounting is so operationally complex, where most calculation errors come from, and how money actually moves through a fund.

Let me start with a mental model that will anchor everything.

Imagine you're sitting at your desk as a controller at a $5 billion private equity fund manager. Your firm manages fourteen different funds across multiple vintages. You have forty limited partners — forty different institutional investors — spread across those funds. Some are pension funds. Some are family offices. Some are insurance companies. Some are endowments. Each one expects distributions on their investment. Each one has slightly different contract terms. And each one will call you the moment they see a number that doesn't match their expectation.

This is the world you're operating in. And to survive in it, you need to understand how closed-end private funds actually work.

So — what is a closed-end private fund? The name tells you most of it. It's closed and it's private.

Closed means the fund has a defined investment period — typically three to five years in PE, five to seven in venture. Once that period closes, the fund stops accepting new commitments. No new money comes in. It's not like a mutual fund where you invest anytime. It's not like a hedge fund that might be open indefinitely. A closed-end fund has a finite life. You commit your capital today, and that commitment is good for the life of the fund — typically ten to twelve years, start to finish.

Private means the fund isn't registered with the SEC. The securities aren't traded on an exchange. These aren't shares you buy and sell publicly. They're held by institutional investors who've committed to the fund through a legal contract.

Two parties matter above all others in a closed-end fund.

The first is the Limited Partner — the LP. The LP is the investor. They commit capital. They have limited liability — which is where the name comes from. If the fund loses money, the LP's downside is capped at what they invested. They can't be sued for more. That's the trade-off. Their upside is also constrained — they participate in profits but don't manage the fund. They trust someone else to do that.

The second party is the General Partner — the GP. The GP is the fund manager. The team of investment professionals who decide what companies to buy, when to buy them, when to sell them, and ultimately how much money the investors get back. The GP has unlimited liability. If something goes badly wrong, the LP can sue the GP. But the GP also participates more heavily in the profits. That's called carried interest — and we're going to spend all of Chapter 7 on that.

So you have this fundamental divide. The LPs have capital and limited liability. The GP has unlimited liability but gets a larger upside. This creates the incentive structure that makes private equity work. The GP is motivated to perform because they're exposed to downside risk and they participate disproportionately in the upside.

Now let's talk about how money flows through one of these funds. Because understanding that flow is the foundation of everything we'll discuss.

Imagine it's January of year zero. The fund is about to launch. The GP has been out raising money and has lined up investors. On January 15th, the fund hits its first close. That means the LP commitments are locked in. Let's say the fund is a billion dollars. The LPs have committed one billion.

Notice I said committed. They have not sent the money yet. They've signed a contract saying they will send money when the GP asks them to.

Here's the first critical distinction. There's committed capital and there's called capital. Committed capital is the promise. Called capital is the actual cash the fund has received. This distinction is going to be the source of most of your accounting headaches if you're not careful.

Three months later, the GP finds a company they want to buy — a software company, price tag seventy-five million. The GP needs capital to make this purchase. So the GP calls for capital. They send out a capital call notice, typically with ten business days of notice. The notice says: "We need seventy-five million for an investment. Please send your portion by this date."

But here's where it gets interesting. The GP doesn't just collect seventy-five million. The GP collects seventy-five million plus the management fees for the quarter plus the fund expenses for the quarter. So the actual capital call might be eighty million. The LPs are pro-rata — based on their commitment percentage. If you're an LP with a ten percent commitment in this billion dollar fund, your commitment is a hundred million. You'll be asked to pay ten percent of the eighty million dollar call — eight million dollars.

You wire eight million to the fund. The fund now has eighty million in cash. Seventy-five million goes to buy the software company. The rest goes to management fees and operating expenses.

This is the capital call cycle. The GP only calls capital when they need it. There's a reason for this. If the GP called all the committed capital upfront, the fund would have a billion dollars sitting in cash earning next to nothing. Wasteful. The GP wants to call capital just in time for investment and minimize the gap between calling it and deploying it.

Fast forward three years. The investment period is ending. The GP has invested most of the committed capital — bought five companies. Some are doing well. Some are not. Now the fund enters what we call the harvest period. The GP focuses on selling companies and returning capital to LPs.

In year four, the GP sells the first company for a profit. Sale proceeds come in — let's say a hundred twenty million. Now the fund has a choice. Distribute it back to the LPs, or reinvest some of it. Eventually the fund has to return capital. And when it does, it calculates the distribution.

Here's the second critical concept. A distribution is not the same as a return. A distribution is money going back to the LPs. Part of that distribution might be return of capital — the LP is getting their own money back. Part might be return on capital — that's profit. The waterfall determines how much is which — and we're spending Chapters 6 and 7 on that.

Let's say from that hundred twenty million dollar sale, the fund distributes a hundred million. The terms say the first part goes back to all LPs proportionally to return their invested capital. Once all LPs have gotten their capital back plus an eight percent annual preferred return, then the GP gets twenty percent of the remaining profit and the LPs get eighty percent. This is carried interest. It's one of the most contentious and important calculations in fund accounting.

Now fast forward to year ten. The fund is winding down. All companies have been sold. All distributions have been made. The GP has calculated the final waterfall, returned capital to the LPs, dissolved the fund. All the regulatory paperwork is filed. The LPs have their final statements.

But here's what you need to understand. This whole process — from day one to day 3,650 — is governed by one document. It's called the Limited Partnership Agreement — the LPA. It's a contract between the GP and the LPs. It specifies everything. The term of the fund. The investment period. The fee structure. How capital calls work. How distributions are calculated. The waterfall. Everything.

And if the LPA isn't clear — or if you misread it — you'll send out a distribution that's wrong. You'll calculate a fee that's wrong. You'll have to restate something. You'll have to fix something. And fixing something in fund accounting is not like fixing a typo. It cascades.

So as you go through this course, you're going to learn how to extract the terms that matter from an LPA and how to make sure your calculations match those terms.

Let me give you the complete lifecycle before we move on.

The fund starts with a fundraising period — maybe eighteen months. The GP meets investors, pitches, raises commitments. The fund hits first close when it has enough to start. But fundraising continues. More investors come in through subsequent closes. Finally — final close. No more commitments accepted.

Then the investment period — three to five years. The GP calls capital and invests it. Takes board seats, improves companies, starts thinking about exits.

As the investment period ends, the fund transitions to the harvest period. The GP sells companies, returns capital, maybe reinvests some proceeds. This period can be three to seven years depending on markets.

Finally — wind-down. Sell remaining assets, calculate final waterfalls, distribute remaining capital, close the fund. One to two years.

Throughout all of this — management fees are being calculated. Capital calls are happening. Distributions are happening. And you, as the accountant or controller, are tracking all of it.

The key thing. A closed-end fund is fundamentally different from an open-end fund or a mutual fund because there is a defined beginning and a defined end. A defined group of investors. And very specific terms governing how money moves.

That's what a closed-end private fund is. Next chapter — we're going to open the LPA and show you what's inside. Specifically, the Limited Partnership Agreement and those side letters that seem simple but absolutely complicate everything.

Part 1: The Industry Chapter 2

Fund Formation

LPAs, Side Letters, Commitment Periods, Fundraising Mechanics

Quick recap. Last chapter — we covered what a closed-end fund is. LPs are the investors. GPs are the managers. Money flows in through capital calls and flows out through distributions. The whole thing runs for about ten years and is governed by a single document.

That document is the LPA. Today we're going to open it up. Because the LPA is the source of truth for everything in fund accounting. Get the LPA terms wrong and everything downstream is wrong.

So what is a Limited Partnership Agreement? It's a contract — essentially a constitution — that governs the fund. Drafted by the GP's counsel, negotiated with the LPs' counsel, signed by all parties. Typically somewhere between fifty and a hundred fifty pages. Dense. Full of legal language. Not something you read for fun.

But here's the thing. You don't have to memorize the whole thing. You have to know where to find the terms that affect accounting and operations. Let me walk you through the sections that matter.

The first section covers basics — fund name, jurisdiction, management company, key personnel. Straightforward. You need to know who the GP is, who can sign things, and where the fund is incorporated because that affects legal treatment.

Next — the term of the fund. This is critical. Typically ten years initially. The GP can usually extend two to three years if they haven't fully exited investments. This matters for financial and tax reporting.

Then the investment period. This is the window during which the GP can make capital calls to invest. Usually shorter than the fund term — a ten-year fund might have a five-year investment period. After year five, no more capital calls for new investments. Only distributions going back. This distinction matters because it affects how management fees are calculated. During the investment period, the fee is often one formula. After — it's a different formula.

The commitment section specifies how much the fund is raising, minimum commitment per LP — usually ten million, sometimes fifty, sometimes a hundred million — and how many closes the fund will have. First close might bring in three hundred million. Second close three months later adds another two hundred million. Final close gets to the target of a billion.

Here's where it gets interesting. Different LPs commit in different closes. And if the fund hasn't hit final close yet, the total fund size is uncertain. An LP might have committed a hundred million at first close expecting the fund to be a billion. But if the fund ultimately raises 1.2 billion — their commitment percentage is different than they thought.

This is managed through equalization. When you have multiple closes, different LPs have different entry points. The LP who committed at first close waited longer for their capital to be deployed. The LP who came in at second close gets deployed sooner. To make this fair, the GP uses equalization to true-up capital activity so everyone's treated as if they'd been in since inception. Equalization is complex — it involves catch-up payments, interest calculations, and retroactive adjustments. The key takeaway is that different LPs can have different cost bases depending on when they committed. This affects waterfall calculations and tax reporting.

Now let's get to the management fees section. This is where things get complicated fast.

The LPA specifies the annual management fee rate — typically one and a half to two percent of committed capital during the investment period. So on a billion dollar fund at two percent, that's twenty million a year — five million per quarter.

Sounds simple. It never stays that way.

The LPA might say two percent of committed capital during the investment period, then one percent of invested capital after. Or two percent of the lower of committed or invested. Or a step-down to one and a half percent in year six. These variations are all negotiated, and they all create different calculation scenarios.

Then there are fee offsets. The LPA might say that transaction fees the GP collects — advisory fees charged to portfolio companies — reduce the management fee. Typically eighty to a hundred percent offsets. So if the GP charges two million in transaction fees on an acquisition, the quarterly management fee might drop by 1.6 million at eighty percent offset.

We'll spend all of Chapter 4 on fee calculations. For now — just know that the management fee section of the LPA is where most of the accounting complexity starts.

The waterfall section. The most important part of the LPA for accounting purposes. The waterfall is the formula that determines how profits get split between LPs and the GP. It specifies tiers — return of capital first, then preferred return, then catch-up, then final split. We're spending all of Chapter 6 on this. For now — just understand that the waterfall is the thing that keeps fund accountants up at night. If it's wrong, every distribution is wrong.

Other sections that matter — key person provisions. If the lead investment partner leaves, the fund might enter wind-down mode. Transfer restrictions — whether LPs can sell their interests. Voting rights — which decisions require LP approval. Default provisions — what happens when an LP doesn't pay a capital call.

Now — you might think the LPA is the complete governance document. You'd be mostly right. But there's another layer. And this is what makes fund accounting so much more complicated than it should be.

Side letters.

A side letter is a separate agreement between the GP and an individual LP. It modifies the LPA terms for that one investor. And here's the operationally painful part — every LP in the fund can have a different side letter.

The fund has forty LPs. The LPA says two percent management fee. But the largest LP — an endowment with a hundred million commitment — negotiated one and a half percent. Another LP, a pension fund, negotiated one and a half percent but only after the investment period. A third LP, a family office, negotiated a co-investment right — they can invest additional capital alongside the fund on certain deals.

Now when you calculate the quarterly management fee, you can't just multiply two percent times committed capital. You have to calculate a separate fee for each LP based on their side letter terms. Forty LPs, potentially forty different calculations per quarter. Over a ten-year fund — sixteen hundred separate fee calculations. If each one is wrong by even a half percent, the cumulative error is enormous.

The most common side letter provisions are fee discounts, preferred return overrides, co-investment rights, and Most Favored Nation clauses — MFN clauses.

An MFN clause is protection for the LP. It says something like: "If you offer any other LP a better fee rate or a better preferred return, we automatically get that same deal." This creates a cascade. Offer a better discount to LP B, and LP A — who has an MFN clause — automatically gets it too. When you negotiate a new LP into the fund, you might inadvertently trigger improvements for existing LPs.

Co-investment rights allow an LP to invest additional capital in specific deals alongside the fund. This is capital above their original commitment, at the same terms. It creates a separate accounting stream for each co-investment.

Let me talk about the commitment period more specifically. The LPA specifies a window during which the GP can make capital calls — typically three to five years in PE. During this time, LPs must respond to calls. After the commitment period ends, the GP can't make new capital calls. The LP's obligation is done.

What happens if an LP doesn't pay a capital call? The LPA specifies default provisions. Typically — penalty interest first, maybe two percent above prime. If they still don't pay — suspension from future calls and distributions. If they really don't pay — the GP can sell their interest or remove them from the fund entirely.

In practice, LP defaults are rare. Most LPs are sophisticated investors who understand consequences. But it happens — especially during financial crises or when an LP has treasury issues.

Now let me talk about fundraising mechanics. The GP goes out, does roadshows, builds a pipeline. This period typically lasts six to eighteen months. When they have enough commitments — first close. The fund becomes legally constituted. The LPA is signed. Maybe three hundred million committed.

They keep raising. Second close three months later — another two hundred million. They keep going. Final close maybe twelve months after first close — a billion total. No more commitments accepted. The fund has a defined investor base.

After final close, the investment period begins. Capital calls start.

Here's the operational bottom line. By the time you're accounting for a fund — the LPA is signed, the side letters are in place, the investor base is set. But you're operating in a world where every LP might have different terms, where there were multiple closes with different equalization mechanics, and where the rules of engagement vary across the entire investor base.

This is why fund accounting is hard. Not because the individual calculations are complex. Because you're doing hundreds of individual calculations simultaneously — each with slightly different rules — and they all have to reconcile back to a total that makes sense.

Part 1: The Industry Chapter 3

Capital Calls

Mechanics, Notice Requirements, Treasury Operations, Defaults

Recap. Chapter 1 — what a closed-end fund is and how money flows. Chapter 2 — the LPA, side letters, and how LPs commit capital.

Now we're at the question that makes fund accounting necessary. The GP has found a company to buy. They need the capital. So they call it. How does a capital call actually work? What's the process? What's the math? What happens when someone doesn't pay?

A capital call is a request from the GP to the LPs for capital. The GP needs cash — for an investment, for fees, for expenses — so they send a notice. The notice specifies how much, when, and what for. Each LP's pro-rata share is included.

Pro-rata share is based on commitment percentage. If the fund is a billion and you committed a hundred million, you're ten percent. When the GP calls for capital, you get called for ten percent of whatever's being called.

Let's work through a real example.

It's March of year one. The fund has forty LPs. Total committed capital — one billion. The GP found a software company and they're acquiring it for a hundred fifty million. But the call isn't just for the acquisition. They also need to cover management fees and operating expenses for the quarter.

Here's the breakdown. A hundred fifty million for the investment. One and a half million in management fees. Half a million in fund operating expenses. Total capital call — a hundred fifty-two million.

The notice goes to all forty LPs. For an LP with a ten percent commitment — their share is fifteen million two hundred thousand. For an LP at two percent — three million forty thousand. For an LP at half a percent — seven hundred sixty thousand.

The LPs wire the funds to a capital call account. Each wire includes a reference number identifying which LP is paying. The fund's administrators track incoming wires and match them to the call. If a wire comes in late or short — the fund tracks that.

Notice period is typically ten business days. That's long enough for an LP's investment committee to approve the wire, for the treasurer to arrange funds, for the wire to clear. Short enough that the GP isn't waiting forever. Every day of delay costs money when you're trying to close an acquisition.

Now — here's a real-world issue that changes the timing game entirely. The acquisition closes on a specific date. The GP might issue the capital call five business days before closing. LPs get the capital to the fund maybe two or three days before closing. That's tight. The fund has to receive the capital, sweep it to a dedicated account, and wire it to the seller at closing.

This is why most large funds use a subscription credit facility. A subscription line — also called a sub line — is a short-term loan backed by the LP commitments. Here's how it works.

The fund gets a line of credit from a bank — typically twenty to thirty percent of total committed capital. So a billion dollar fund might have a three hundred million dollar sub line. When the GP needs capital immediately, the fund borrows from the sub line instead of waiting for LPs to respond. Capital is available same day. The GP closes the acquisition. Then the fund issues a capital call to pay back the sub line. LPs respond to the call, borrowed funds get repaid.

The sub line solves the timing problem. It lets the GP deploy capital immediately while giving LPs the standard ten-day notice period.

The cost — interest on the borrowed amount, typically three to four percent above prime. Plus a commitment fee on the undrawn portion, usually twenty-five to fifty basis points per year. These costs are fund expenses that reduce LP returns. But without the sub line, the fund would be uncompetitive — sitting around waiting for wires while deals close.

Now let's talk about what happens when an LP doesn't pay.

An LP is in default if they don't pay within the notice period plus a cure period — typically five to ten business days. So maybe fifteen to twenty business days total.

First action — penalty interest. The LPA might say two percent above prime. If prime is five percent, the LP pays seven percent on the unpaid amount.

Still don't pay — suspension. No future calls, no distributions. Capital frozen. This usually motivates payment pretty quickly.

Still nothing — default proceedings. The GP can sell the LP's interest to another investor or return it to the fund.

In practice — defaults are rare. But they happen. Let me give you an example.

It's June. The fund issues a two hundred thirty-three million dollar call. Forty LPs get their notices. Most wire on day eight or nine. By the deadline, thirty-nine have paid. One LP — a sovereign wealth fund with a two percent commitment — hasn't paid. No communication. They're in default.

Cure notice goes out. Five business days to pay four million six hundred sixty thousand, or default proceedings begin. On day three of the cure period — the wire arrives. Their investment committee delayed approval. It happens.

But by now the acquisition was supposed to have closed. So what happened? The fund used the sub line. They borrowed the full amount on the day the capital call was issued, closed the acquisition on schedule, and when the LP's cure payment arrived, they used it to pay down the sub line. The borrowing was outstanding for just over a week. Cost the fund about a hundred thousand in interest. Cost of doing business.

Here's another complexity. Not every LP is called for the same amount. Side letters — remember those from Chapter 2? Some LPs have terms that affect their capital calls.

An LP with a co-investment right might invest additional capital beyond their main commitment on certain deals. The fund has to track co-investment capital separately. That's a separate accounting stream.

An LP might have negotiated a capital call cap — their calls can't exceed fifty million per quarter. If the call would exceed that, they pay the cap and the fund has to make up the difference somewhere.

An LP might have negotiated that they don't participate in certain deal types. If the call is for a deal they've opted out of, their share has to be reallocated to other LPs.

These variations are why capital call administration is complex in funds with lots of side letters. Each LP might have a different call amount. A spreadsheet can handle a few variations. When you have forty LPs with fifteen different side letter provisions, the spreadsheet starts breaking.

One more scenario worth understanding. Sometimes capital calls happen for reasons other than investments. The fund might call capital to pay a contingent liability. Or to cover a sub line draw that hasn't been repaid. Or — and this one sounds odd — to fund a distribution.

How can you distribute capital if you need to call capital? This happens when the fund is distributing on a mark-to-market basis. The fund might have a paper gain on a company it hasn't sold yet, but the LPA allows distributions based on marked value. The fund calculates the distribution, then calls capital to fund it. Less common, but it happens.

The operational takeaway. A capital call is not a simple process. You need to identify what capital is needed. Calculate the pro-rata share for each LP. Adjust for side letters. Track payments. Manage defaults. Reconcile against the sub line if one's being used. You need systems to do this — because spreadsheets will break under the weight of the complexity.

Management Fees

Fee Structures, Calculation Mechanics, Offsets, Complexity

Recap. We've covered fund structure, the LPA, and capital calls. Now we're going to talk about how the GP gets paid. And I want you to understand something upfront — management fees sound simple. They are not. This is one of the most error-prone areas in fund accounting.

Every LP understands the GP needs to be paid. Office space, investment professionals, accountants, lawyers, software — it costs money. So LPs agree to pay a management fee. The standard structure is a percentage of committed capital during the investment period, switching to a different basis after. Typically one and a half to two percent during, one to one and a half percent after.

That's the headline. Now let me show you why the headline is misleading.

Let's start with a concrete example. Mid-market PE fund. One billion dollars. The LPA says two percent of committed capital during the investment period. Investment period is years zero through five. Fee calculated quarterly — two percent divided by four gives you half a percent per quarter times a billion. That's five million per quarter. Twenty million per year.

Simple, right?

Now let's add complexity. One layer at a time.

Layer one — the fee basis switch. Years six through ten, the fee changes to one and a half percent of invested capital. Invested capital is different from committed capital. Invested capital is what the fund has actually deployed. On day one it's zero. By year five, maybe they've deployed eight hundred million. So the fee in year six is one and a half percent of eight hundred million — twelve million per year. Significant drop from twenty million.

But what about the two hundred million that was committed but never invested? Some funds drop the fee on uninvested capital to zero. Some charge a reduced rate. Some continue at a nominal amount. The LPA has to specify. If it's ambiguous — different investors will interpret it differently. And then you have a problem.

Layer two — step-downs. Many LPAs include them. Two percent in years one through five. One and a half in year six. One and a half of invested capital in years seven through nine. One percent of invested capital in year ten. Now you have to recalculate the fee every quarter based on where you are in the fund lifecycle.

Layer three — fee holidays. Some GPs waive fees for a period. Maybe zero percent for the first two quarters to attract capital. Then two percent kicks in. You need to track when the holiday starts and ends.

Layer four — side letters. The pension fund LP negotiated one and a half percent instead of two. The endowment negotiated a co-investment fee waiver on co-invested capital but pays two percent on the main commitment. Now you're calculating different rates for different LPs.

Layer five — offsets. Transaction fees the GP collects from portfolio companies reduce the management fee. Eighty percent offset, typically. So if the GP charges five million in transaction fees in a quarter, the management fee drops by four million. But — some offsets apply only to certain fee types. Transaction fees offset at eighty percent. Monitoring fees at fifty percent. Exit fees might not offset at all. You have to track which fees are which and apply the right offset to each.

Are you starting to see the problem?

You have a base fee with multiple modifiers. Side letters with different rates per LP. Step-downs that change the formula. Holidays that eliminate fees for periods. Offsets that reduce fees based on other activities. Different offset percentages for different fee types. And you have to calculate this every single quarter for every single LP.

Forty LPs times forty quarters over a ten-year fund — that's sixteen hundred separate calculations. If each one is wrong by even a small amount, the cumulative error compounds.

Let me walk through a complete quarterly fee calculation so you can feel the full weight of this.

December of year three. The fund has forty LPs. Total committed capital — one billion. Invested capital — six hundred fifty million. The LPA says two percent of committed capital during the investment period with a step-down in year six. Transaction fees offset at eighty percent.

In Q4 of year three, the fund collected five million in transaction fees from the portfolio. So — base quarterly fee is five million. Transaction fee offset — eighty percent of five million is four million. Net quarterly fee — one million.

But now the side letters. Thirty-eight LPs pay the standard two percent. Two LPs negotiated discounts. The pension fund pays one and a half percent. The endowment pays two percent on their main commitment but has a co-investment waiver.

Each group's fee has to be calculated separately. Then the offset has to be allocated. How is the offset allocated across LPs? There are two ways — allocate pro-rata based on each LP's share of the total fee, or apply the offset against the total fund fee first and then allocate the net.

The LPA needs to specify this. If it doesn't — you're guessing. And guessing in fund accounting is how restatements happen.

Now let's add another scenario. Year seven. Investment period ended in year five. The fee switches to invested capital. But invested capital has been declining as the fund exits companies. By year seven, invested capital is four hundred million — down from the peak of eight hundred million. The fee is now one and a half percent of four hundred million — six million per year. That's a big drop from the twenty million the GP was collecting during the investment period.

Some funds have a fee floor — the fee can't drop below a certain amount, like five million per year, no matter how low invested capital gets. This protects the GP from having fees crater during the harvest period.

Others have a fee cap — the fee can't exceed a certain amount even if committed capital is very large. These variations all have to be coded into your calculation model.

Here's the operational takeaway. You need a system that can handle multi-tier fee calculations. A spreadsheet can work — but it becomes a maze of formulas and lookup tables. Many funds use specialized systems that configure fee calculations per fund and per LP.

Without that system, your team will spend too much time recalculating every quarter. And they'll make mistakes. Fees are reconciled to the LPs every quarter on their statements. If your fee numbers are wrong — the LPs will call.

The calculation that sounds like it should take five minutes takes hours when you account for side letters, offsets, step-downs, holidays, and LP-specific variations. This is one of the core problems Maybern was built to solve — but we'll get to that in Part 3.

Distributions

Return of Capital, Return on Capital, Tax Distributions, Recycling

We've spent four chapters talking about money flowing into the fund. Capital calls. Management fees. All cash going from LPs to the fund. Now we flip it. Money's going the other direction. The fund is paying LPs back.

A distribution is money going from the fund back to the Limited Partners. And the first thing you need to understand is that not all distribution dollars are created equal.

There are two fundamentally different types of money inside a distribution.

The first is return of capital. This is the investor's own money coming back. If an LP invested a hundred million and the fund distributes fifty million to them — that fifty million is return of capital. The LP is getting their money back. Not profit. Just a partial recovery of what they put in.

The second is return on capital. This is profit. It's the earnings on what the LP invested. If the LP put in a hundred million for a company now worth a hundred fifty million — there's fifty million in gains. When that gain is distributed, it's return on capital.

These two types are treated very differently. Return of capital is typically not taxable to the LP as ordinary income. Return on capital is taxable. For the LP's tax planning, they need to know exactly how much of their distribution is each type. Get it wrong and you're creating tax headaches for sophisticated institutional investors who have their own compliance teams. They will notice.

The waterfall determines the split — we'll get to that in Chapter 6. For now, understand that the character of every dollar in a distribution matters.

Let me walk through the distribution process.

The fund exits an investment. They sold a company. Proceeds come in. Now the fund calculates the amount available for distribution. That's gross proceeds minus transaction costs, minus debt repayment, minus fund expenses. What's left is distributable.

Say the fund exits for a billion dollars. Fifty million in transaction fees and debt repayment. Nine hundred fifty million available for distribution.

The fund has a choice — distribute all of it, or hold some back for reinvestment or liquidity. The LPA typically gives the GP discretion. Let's say the GP distributes all nine hundred fifty million.

Now the waterfall runs. First tier — return all capital LPs invested. Second tier — preferred return. Third tier — GP catch-up. Fourth tier — final split. The waterfall determines what each LP gets. And each LP might get a different result because of side letter terms.

Once the waterfall is calculated, the fund determines each LP's share. For LP One at ten percent with no side letters — their share is ninety-five million. Maybe sixty million is return of capital and thirty-five million is profit.

The fund issues a distribution notice to all LPs. Date of distribution. Amount per LP. How much is return of capital versus profit. Wire instructions. Distributions are typically wired within two to five business days of the exit. The LPs receive funds. They record them. They report on their tax filings.

Now — not every LP is eligible for every distribution. Some might be in default on a capital call — their distributions get suspended until they cure. Some might have different vesting periods. Some might have preferred distribution terms from their side letters. Some might have co-investments with separate distribution schedules.

This means the distribution list has to be customized. It's not "divide by forty." It's a complex calculation for each LP based on their eligibility, their terms, and their current status.

Let me talk about a specific scenario that's more common than people expect. Tax distributions.

Here's the problem they solve. In a fund structured as a partnership, the fund itself doesn't pay taxes. The LPs pay taxes. The fund makes profits, those profits flow through to the LPs as partnership income. The LPs owe taxes on those profits — even if the fund hasn't distributed the money yet.

Think about that. The fund buys a company for a hundred million. Two years later it's worth a hundred fifty million. From an accounting perspective — fifty million dollar gain. The LPs are allocated their share. An LP with ten percent is allocated five million in gains.

From a tax perspective — that five million is income. The LP owes taxes on it. But the fund hasn't sold the company. The LP hasn't received a dime. They have a tax bill on income they never got. This is called phantom income. And LPs hate it.

To solve this, many funds distribute cash periodically to help LPs pay taxes on phantom income. Tax distributions. The fund calculates how much in gains each LP has been allocated since the last distribution, then sends cash — or some percentage of it — to cover the tax obligation.

Mechanically — the fund needs cash available. Maybe from earlier exits. Maybe from the sub line. The fund calculates the amount, issues a notice, wires the funds. Later, when the investment is actually sold, the tax distribution is credited against the eventual distribution. The LP doesn't get double-paid.

Now — net distributions. This is a situation where a capital call and a distribution happen in the same event.

When would this happen? The fund realizes a gain on one company but wants to reinvest some of the gain into a new opportunity. But the new opportunity is outside the investment period, so technically the LP should approve capital redeployment. To simplify — the fund issues a net distribution. "We're distributing the gain to you, but we're also calling that capital back to reinvest." The net effect — your capital stays in the fund, you receive the gain. Fewer transactions, less administrative work.

Recycling. This is when the fund redeploys distributed capital back into new investments during the investment period.

The LPA specifies whether recycling is allowed. If it is — when the fund distributes proceeds from an early exit, that capital can be reinvested into new deals. Without recycling, exited capital sits idle. With recycling, the fund can keep deploying.

Recycling is valuable because it extends the fund's investment activity. But it also means the LP's capital is at risk for longer. And it complicates the waterfall because capital has been returned and then redeployed — you have to track what counts as "returned" versus "redeployed" for purposes of calculating preferred return.

Let me talk about the timeline of a distribution. People always ask — "when will I get my money?"

It's not as fast as you'd think. The fund exits on a Tuesday. Proceeds arrive Wednesday. The fund calculates the distribution — including running the waterfall. If there are side letter terms with different waterfall calculations per LP, this takes time. Maybe by Thursday the distribution is calculated.

Notices get prepared. Reviewed by legal or compliance. By Friday they're sent to LPs. Payment date — next Wednesday. Wires go out. LPs receive funds over the next two business days. By the following Friday — done.

Best case — two weeks from exit to receipt. If it's complex — longer. I've seen distributions take a month to process. I've seen disputes about distribution amounts that take three months to resolve.

The biggest operational challenge with distributions is reconciliation. Each LP is entitled to a specific amount. Calculate it wrong and you're either clawing back money you shouldn't have sent — which is awkward — or you're underpaying someone who's going to call, email, and get frustrated.

Most fund accounting errors come from distribution mistakes. A waterfall was calculated wrong. A side letter wasn't applied. A fee offset reduced the distribution more than it should have. This is why detailed policies and checkpoints are critical. Someone calculates. Someone else reviews. The GP approves. Only then do wires go out.

The Waterfall

European vs. American, Calculation Logic, Clawbacks

This is it. The most important and most complex calculation in fund accounting. The waterfall.

The waterfall is the formula that determines how profits get split between LPs and the GP. It's called a waterfall because money flows down from one tier to the next. First tier fills up. Overflows to the second. Second fills. Overflows to the third. And so on.

The waterfall determines the character of every distribution. If it's wrong — every distribution is wrong. Most fund accounting mistakes I've seen are waterfall errors. Misplaced decimal points. Misunderstood preferred return calculations. Failure to track carry at the individual LP level. Clawbacks calculated wrong.

Let me walk you through it.

There are two fundamentally different waterfall structures. The European waterfall — also called the whole-fund waterfall. And the American waterfall — also called the deal-by-deal waterfall. Many funds use a hybrid. But let's start with the pure versions.

The European waterfall calculates profits at the fund level. All capital is returned to all LPs before the GP gets any carry.

Here's how it works. The fund is being liquidated. Deployed eight hundred million in capital. Portfolio is now worth 1.2 billion. Net gain — four hundred million.

Tier one — return of capital. The LPs invested eight hundred million. That eight hundred million flows back to them first, pro-rata by commitment percentage. An LP with ten percent gets eighty million.

Tier two — preferred return. Typically eight percent annually on the capital that was returned. Capital was deployed over a five-year investment period, average holding period maybe three years. Let's say the preferred return across all LPs totals a hundred ninety-two million. This flows to the LPs.

So far the LPs have received eight hundred million in capital back plus a hundred ninety-two million in preferred return — nine hundred ninety-two million total. Fund proceeds are 1.2 billion. That leaves two hundred eight million.

Tier three — GP catch-up. The GP wants to make sure they receive twenty percent of total profits. Total profits are four hundred million. Twenty percent is eighty million. The GP has received zero so far. So eighty million flows to the GP in catch-up.

Remaining after catch-up — a hundred twenty-eight million.

Tier four — final split. Eighty-twenty. LPs get eighty percent — a hundred two point four million. GP gets twenty percent — twenty-five point six million.

Final tally. LPs received nine hundred ninety-two million plus a hundred two point four million — roughly 1.094 billion. GP received eighty million plus twenty-five point six million — roughly a hundred five point six million. Total — 1.2 billion. Checks out.

Now the American waterfall — deal-by-deal.

In this structure, the waterfall is calculated separately for each investment. The fund made three investments. Investment A — deployed a hundred million, exited at six hundred million, five hundred million gain. Investment B — deployed two hundred million, exited at three hundred fifty million, a hundred fifty million gain. Investment C — deployed five hundred million, exited at four hundred million, a hundred million loss.

In a deal-by-deal waterfall, you run the waterfall for each deal independently. Investment A — return capital, pay preferred return, calculate carry. Investment B — same process. Investment C — it's a loss, so LPs absorb it.

The advantage — profitable deals can fund carry earlier. The GP gets paid on winners without waiting for the entire fund to settle. This is generally better for the GP.

The disadvantage — the GP might collect carry on early winners even if the overall fund eventually loses money. Which leads us to clawback.

Hard hurdle versus soft hurdle. Important distinction.

A hard hurdle means the GP doesn't get any carry until LPs have received their full preferred return. A soft hurdle means the GP can start receiving carry once the preferred return is being paid — through the catch-up mechanism we just described.

In our European example, we used a soft hurdle. The GP caught up to twenty percent of total profits through the catch-up tier. In a hard hurdle, the GP would only earn carry on profits above the preferred return — no catch-up. Hard hurdles are less common because GPs don't like them. But some funds use them to be more investor-friendly.

Now — clawback. One of the most important and most misunderstood concepts in fund accounting.

Clawback is the GP's obligation to return excess carry at the end of the fund. Here's why it exists.

During the fund's life, the GP collects carry on distributions of gains. But the GP doesn't know if the fund will ultimately be profitable overall. Early investments might be wildly profitable — GP collects carry. Later investments might lose money — the overall fund underperforms. At the end of the fund, the final waterfall is calculated on all capital. If the GP received more carry than the final waterfall justifies — they have to return the excess.

Example. Year three — the GP exits Investment A for a five hundred million gain. GP receives fifty million in carry. Year seven — Investment C exits for a hundred million loss. Year ten — the fund winds down. Overall the fund invested a billion and realized nine hundred million. Net loss — a hundred million.

With a net loss, the GP gets zero carry in the final waterfall. But they already received fifty million from Investment A. They owe clawback. They have to return that fifty million.

Operationally — this is complex. The GP already distributed much of that carry as compensation to the deal team. Asking for it back is painful. This is why many funds have escrow arrangements — a portion of carry is held in escrow until the fund concludes. Only then does it get released.

Some funds have non-clawbackable carry provisions. Maybe fifty percent of carry can't be clawed back. So if the GP received fifty million and half is non-clawbackable — they only owe twenty-five million in clawback. LPA has to specify.

Now let's talk about side letters and the waterfall. This is where it gets truly complex.

If every LP has the same terms — the waterfall is hard but manageable. You calculate the tiers and allocate pro-rata. But if different LPs have different preferred return rates, different carry rates, different terms — the calculation becomes exponentially harder.

LP One negotiated nine percent preferred return instead of eight. LP Two negotiated ninety percent of profits instead of eighty. Each LP's waterfall has to be tracked individually.

With forty LPs and potentially different terms for each — the waterfall becomes essentially impossible in Excel. You need specialized systems that can model each LP's individual waterfall and aggregate them into a fund total.

This is exactly why fund accounting platforms and firms like Maybern exist. The calculation is too complex for standard tools.

Here's the critical operational insight. The waterfall is forward-looking. Every time the fund makes a distribution, you calculate the waterfall based on what you know so far. But the fund isn't finished. There might be more investments. More exits. The final waterfall gets calculated at the very end of the fund's life.

So the carry you distribute now might not be justified by the final fund performance. That's why clawback exists. That's why tracking carry through the fund's life is essential. And that's why the waterfall is the calculation that keeps everyone honest.

You need clear written policies on how your fund calculates waterfalls. Those policies have to match the LPA and any side letter terms. You need a system that implements those policies without human error. And you need to review every waterfall calculation before it goes to investors.

Carried Interest

What It Is, How It Works, Why It's Complicated

Last chapter we talked about the waterfall — the formula that splits profits between LPs and the GP. Now we're going to zoom in on one specific component. Carried interest. Carry.

Carry is the GP's share of the profits. It's called "carry" because the GP doesn't put up capital for this share. The LPs put up capital. The GP invests it, manages it, and in return gets a portion of the profits. This is the GP's compensation beyond the management fee. And it's where things get interesting — both operationally and politically.

Typically carry is twenty percent. Once LPs have received their capital back plus their preferred return, the GP gets twenty percent of remaining profits and the LPs get eighty. But carry rates vary. Some funds negotiate fifteen percent. Some get twenty-five. The rate is set at fund formation and specified in the LPA.

How is carry earned? Through the waterfall. When the fund exits an investment and distributes proceeds, the waterfall determines how much is carry. Let's walk through it.

The fund exits a company for a three hundred million dollar gain. Waterfall kicks in. First tier — return of capital to LPs. Let's say that requires a hundred million. Second tier — preferred return. Another fifty million to LPs. That leaves a hundred fifty million.

GP catch-up tier. The GP needs to receive twenty percent of total profits — which is sixty million. GP has zero so far. So sixty million flows to the GP.

Ninety million remains. Final tier — eighty-twenty. LPs get seventy-two million. GP gets eighteen million.

Total carry for the GP — sixty million in catch-up plus eighteen million in the final split — seventy-eight million. That's carry.

But that seventy-eight million doesn't all belong to the GP partners personally. It belongs to the GP entity. The GP firm then distributes it to the deal team based on their contribution to the investment.

This is where carry allocation gets complex. The GP has ten investment partners. The lead partner on this deal might get forty percent. The secondary partner gets thirty. Third partner gets twenty. The rest share ten percent.

Each of these allocations might have vesting. The lead partner's share vests immediately. The secondary partner's might vest over four years — twenty-five percent immediately, the rest monthly. The third partner might have a clawback if they leave the firm within two years.

Now multiply that by twenty investments. You could have two hundred individual carry allocations, each with different vesting terms. All need to be tracked.

The fund accounting team tracks carry at two levels. At the fund level — how much carry the GP earns from each distribution. That's the waterfall. At the GP firm level — how carry is allocated to partners and how much has vested. The fund may not be responsible for the internal allocation — but it is responsible for getting the fund-level number right.

Let me talk about the tax treatment — because this is where carry becomes political.

If carry is structured as a partnership interest in the fund, it's typically taxed at long-term capital gains rates. Long-term capital gains rates are lower than ordinary income rates. This is the famous "carried interest loophole."

The controversy is straightforward. The GP is providing services — managing investments, making decisions. That looks like compensation, which should be taxed at ordinary income rates. But because carry is structured as a partnership interest, it gets capital gains treatment. This has been a political football for years. There have been proposals to change it. But carry structured as partnership interest still gets capital gains rates.

This matters to LPs because it affects the total deal economics. If the GP is paying lower taxes on carry, the GP's effective take is higher. From the LP's perspective, that's a redistribution of value.

GP commitment. Most funds require the GP to have skin in the game. The GP commits their own capital — typically one to five percent of total fund capital. A billion dollar fund, the GP puts in fifty million. That capital is invested alongside the LPs and treated the same way initially. But when profits are distributed, the GP receives carry on top of their co-investment returns.

From an accounting perspective, this creates a nuance. The GP has committed capital and they also receive carry. When the waterfall runs, you exclude the GP's committed capital from the LP pool, calculate LP shares, then allocate carry to the GP separately. The GP is both an investor and a profit participant.

Let me give you a real-world scenario that shows why carry tracking is complex.

The fund is ten years old. Twenty investments made over its life. Fifteen exited. Five still in portfolio.

From the fifteen exits, the GP received two hundred million in carry. Of this — a hundred million was non-clawbackable under the LPA.

Now the fund is winding down. The remaining five investments are being sold. Market conditions have deteriorated. By the time all five are sold, the fund has lost fifty million on them.

Final waterfall. Total capital deployed — one billion. Total proceeds — nine hundred fifty million. Net loss — fifty million.

With a net loss — there's no carry. The GP should have received zero. But they already received two hundred million over the fund's life. Of that, a hundred million is non-clawbackable. So the clawback is a hundred million.

But the GP already distributed much of that carry to the deal team as compensation. Does the team owe clawback? The LPA should specify — but it's contentious. If the team already spent it or paid taxes on it, asking for it back is extremely difficult. This is why escrow arrangements exist — and why many GPs are moving toward holding carry in escrow until the fund concludes.

Here's the operational takeaway on carry. You need a system that can do the following:

Calculate fund-level carry through the waterfall. Allocate that carry to the GP. Track which portion is non-clawbackable. For each distribution during the fund's life, record how much carry was distributed. At the end of the fund, calculate the final waterfall and determine if clawback is owed.

If the GP allocates carry internally — you need to track that for compensation and tax reporting too.

This is complex and error-prone. Many fund accounting systems now have dedicated carry tracking modules because it's such a critical piece. And it's one of the core workflows Maybern was built around — but that's Part 3 territory.

Side Letters and Investor-Level Economics

Why Every LP Is Different, and What That Means for Your Numbers

We've spent seven chapters building the complete picture. Fund structure. LPAs. Capital calls. Fees. Distributions. Waterfalls. Carry. Now we're going to come back to the thing that complicates all of it — and treat it as the operational challenge it really is.

Side letters and investor-level economics.

Here's the fundamental principle that makes fund accounting complicated. Every LP in a fund can have different economic terms. The LPA sets the base. But any LP can negotiate a side letter that modifies those terms for themselves.

This is standard in the industry. Large institutional investor commits significant capital — they negotiate. "We'll commit five hundred million, but we want better terms." The GP agrees. Terms get documented in a side letter.

Why does an LP want a side letter? What are they negotiating for? And what does it mean for your accounting?

The most common provision — fee discounts. "We're committing a large amount. We want a discount on management fees." Instead of two percent, they pay one and a half. This saves them millions over the fund's life. From the fund's perspective — reduced fee revenue. Which means when you calculate total fund fees, you can't use a single rate. Every LP might have a different rate.

Next — preferred return overrides. An LP negotiates nine percent instead of eight. Or they negotiate that their preferred return is calculated differently — maybe on a net basis where certain fees are deducted before the preferred return calculation. This affects the waterfall. Higher preferred return means more distribution to that LP before the GP gets carry.

Co-investment rights. An LP negotiates the ability to invest additional capital alongside the fund on certain deals. They commit a billion to the fund. Side letter says on any deal where they choose to participate, they can invest up to an additional hundred million. The co-investment is capital above their commitment, at the same terms. This creates a separate accounting stream — co-investment capital tracked separately from main fund capital.

MFN clauses — Most Favored Nation. This is protection for the LP. "If you offer any other LP a better fee rate, better preferred return, or any other better term — we automatically get it too."

Here's why this matters operationally. You negotiate a new LP into the fund and offer them better terms than existing LPs. If you're not careful — you trigger MFN clauses in existing side letters. Suddenly existing LPs are entitled to the same better terms. Your economics just changed for everybody.

This is why side letters have to be tracked meticulously. You need a registry of all terms for all LPs. When you negotiate new terms, you review existing side letters for MFN triggers.

Now let's talk about what this means for your calculations. Everything gets harder.

Management fees. You can't multiply one rate times committed capital. You look up each LP's rate from their side letter — or determine they're on the standard rate — and apply it individually. Forty LPs, potentially forty different fee calculations per quarter.

Distributions and the waterfall. If different LPs have different preferred returns or different carry rates, you might have to calculate separate waterfalls for different groups. LP One and Two get nine percent preferred return. Everyone else gets eight. You calculate the waterfall in stages — satisfy the eight percent for the majority, then the additional one percent for the preferred group, then handle carry. Cascading waterfalls. Complex and error-prone.

Capital calls. Some LPs might have call caps. Some might have opt-out rights on certain deal types. Some might have co-investment provisions that change their call amounts. The capital call isn't "divide by forty" — it's forty individual calculations.

Let me walk through a realistic example.

One billion dollar fund. Forty LPs. Standard terms — two percent management fee, eight percent preferred return. Here's the side letter overlay:

LP One — ten percent commitment — negotiated one and a half percent fees and nine percent preferred return. LP Two — five percent commitment — negotiated one and a half percent fees, kept eight percent preferred return. LP Three — three percent commitment — negotiated co-investment right of up to fifty million per deal. LP Four — one percent commitment — has an MFN clause that gets them the best terms offered to anyone.

LP Four's MFN is triggered by the fee discounts offered to LPs One and Two. So LP Four gets one and a half percent fees too. Even though LP Four committed the least.

The other thirty-six LPs take standard terms.

When you calculate the quarterly management fee — LP One pays one and a half percent on their commitment. LP Two pays one and a half percent on theirs. LP Four, via MFN, pays one and a half percent. LP Three pays two percent. The other thirty-six pay two percent. Each one calculated separately.

When you calculate a distribution — LP One's nine percent preferred return means they get more before the GP gets carry. Everyone else at eight percent. LP Three's co-investment might participate in the distribution alongside their main commitment — tracked separately.

Every calculation just got multiplied by the number of side letter variations.

Here's the operational nightmare at scale. Imagine you go from one fund to two. Now you have two funds, each with potentially different LPs and different side letter terms. Forty LPs in Fund One. Fifty in Fund Two. Ninety total. Each might have different terms.

Two hundred forty separate management fee calculations per quarter. Separate waterfall calculations for each distribution. A spreadsheet model that worked for one fund with forty LPs cannot scale to two funds with ninety. You need a system.

The GP's perspective on side letters is pragmatic. They negotiate them to secure capital. Large LP wants better terms? Agree to it if it gets the commitment. But the GP has to manage the complexity this creates.

Some GPs are strict. "We'll give you a fee discount but we won't change carry or the waterfall." This limits variation and makes accounting manageable.

Others are flexible. They'll negotiate almost anything. More complexity — but also more competitive in fundraising.

Side letters are also confidential. LP One's terms are between LP One and the GP. Other LPs shouldn't see them. Which creates an administrative burden — the accounting team has to be careful not to disclose one LP's terms to another.

The solution is systematic tracking. A side letter registry or database that captures key terms for each LP. Fee rates. Preferred return rates. Carry rates. Co-investment rights. MFN clauses. Any other material terms. When the team calculates fees or distributions, they check the registry. When a new LP is negotiated, they review existing side letters for MFN triggers.

Fund Administrators

The Gatekeepers of Truth and the Shadow Books Problem

Let me start with a question. Who actually maintains the official books and records of your fund? If you're thinking "our finance team" — you're partially right. But the real answer is more complicated. Behind almost every alternative investment firm sits a third-party fund administrator. Companies like Citco, SS&C, State Street, Gen II, Alter Domus, Ultimus. These firms are the custodians of truth in the alternative fund world. They're not glamorous. They're not household names. But they're absolutely critical to how your fund operates.

Let me explain what a fund administrator actually does — because it's broader than most people think.

Yes, they maintain the official books and records. But they're also calculating Net Asset Value — NAV — the value of your fund at any given point in time. They handle investor communications. Send out capital call notices and distribution notices. Process those calls and distributions. Manage tax reporting — preparing K-1s for LPs, handling foreign tax documentation. Reconcile bank accounts. Produce financial statements. They're the operational backbone.

Think of them as the custodian function. They're holding the cash. Reconciling the securities. Making sure nothing gets lost. Keeping the official ledger of what every investor owns.

Now here's where it gets interesting. And this is what separates theory from practice.

There's a regulatory requirement called the maker-checker principle. Simple idea — one party "makes" something, another party independently "checks" it. In the fund world, the admin "makes" the books. The GP — the fund manager — must "check" them.

This isn't optional. It's not a suggestion from auditors. It's a legal requirement. You can't have the same party both creating and validating financial records. It violates basic internal controls.

But here's the problem. This is where elegant theory meets messy reality. And the meeting point is Excel.

Let me walk you through what the actual day-to-day looks like.

Part 2: The Ecosystem Chapter 11

Real Estate Fund Specifics

When Leverage, Appraisals, and Property Structures Complicate Everything

Real estate funds are their own category within alternatives. And the complexity isn't additive — it's multiplicative. If you're managing a PE fund or a hedge fund, you've got one set of problems. Add real estate to the mix, and you've got entirely different challenges layered on top of everything we've already discussed.

First — the structural difference. In a typical PE fund, you invest in companies. Those companies have market values — or at least comparable company valuations you can reference. Public company multiples. Recent transaction prices. With enough data, you can mark an investment to a reasonable estimate.

Real estate doesn't work that way. Real estate is valued based on appraisals. A professional third-party appraiser looks at the property, examines comparable sales, analyzes the income it generates, and produces a number. That appraisal is the basis for the fund's NAV.

If you're running a fund with ten properties — you need ten appraisals. Fifty properties — fifty appraisals. Each appraiser has their own schedule, their own biases. Some are conservative and tend to undervalue. Some are optimistic. Every single appraisal directly impacts what your investors think their capital is worth. Miss one and you're flying blind. Get an unexpectedly low appraisal and suddenly the fund's reported value drops. Investors get nervous.

Second — leverage. Many real estate funds use debt. They borrow against the properties they own because it magnifies returns. This is different from how most other alternative assets work.

Say you buy a property for a hundred million and borrow sixty million. Your equity is forty million. If the property generates ten million in profit, your leveraged return on the forty million equity is twenty-five percent. But the unlevered return — the return on the full hundred million as if you had no debt — is ten percent. Very different numbers.

An LP might want to see both. Levered return — what they actually experienced. Unlevered return — what the underlying asset did independent of financing decisions. Managing two return tracks means tracking the same property's performance twice with different capital structure assumptions. It means running your waterfall twice — once for the levered world the LP experiences, once for the unlevered world that shows underlying performance.

Third — entity structure. Most real estate funds don't hold properties directly. Each property sits in its own separate legal entity — often called an SOI entity, for Statement of Investment. Fund ABC might own fifty properties, which means fifty subsidiary entities, each owning one property.

Why? Liability protection — if one property gets sued, the others are insulated. Tax efficiency — each entity can be structured for optimal tax treatment. Flexibility — refinance or sell one property without touching the others.

But from an accounting perspective — it's a reporting nightmare. You're doing consolidated financial reporting at the fund level, rolling up fifty entities into one financial statement. Tracking each property's performance and debt separately. If property seven has a balance sheet issue, you have to find it among fifty entities. If the property-level P&L doesn't match expectations, you trace it through the SOI back to the fund level.

Now add international real estate. Multi-currency complexity that is genuinely gnarly.

Your fund raises capital in US dollars. You're buying an office building in London for fifty million pounds. Your fund has dollars. So you convert, buy the building, and then track performance in pounds while your investors want to see returns in dollars.

This means currency translation at acquisition — you locked in an exchange rate the day you bought. Currency translation at valuation — if the pound moved relative to the dollar, the property's dollar value changed even if the pound value didn't. And currency hedge tracking if you're hedging the exposure.

Some investors might be fully hedged — their returns are protected from currency swings. Others partially hedged. Others not hedged at all. So you're tracking multiple investor positions in the same property with different currency exposure and hedge ratios. One investor sees a dollar return. Another sees a pound return. A third sees something in between.

The fund's accounting has to split the property's performance — part flowing at the current exchange rate, part at the hedged rate. And if the hedge is imperfect or gets renewed, the numbers shift. You track gain or loss on the hedge itself, separate from the property's operational performance. A property can generate great returns, but if the hedge is costing money, the net return to the hedged investor is lower.

Add credit facilities. Real estate funds often borrow through credit lines rather than traditional mortgages. A facility might be a hundred million dollar line with multiple tranches — first forty million at a lower rate, remaining sixty million at a higher rate. Different properties might use capital from different tranches.

When calculating carry or distributions, you need to know which debt financed which asset. Property A financed with cheap tranche-one debt has a better return than Property B financed with expensive tranche-two debt. That affects allocation.

Then there are reclassification workflows. When the fund draws on a facility, the capital might start as working capital — cash for operations. Once deployed into a property, it's reclassified as investment capital. But reclassification might change how fees are calculated or how carry is allocated. Some funds charge management fees on invested capital but not on undeployed cash. So a reclassification isn't just an accounting entry — it's a business event that triggers downstream recalculations. In spreadsheets, easy to forget. Downstream reconciliation errors follow.

Most real estate firms use Yardi as their property management and GL system. Yardi is powerful at the property level. Maintenance costs. Leasing records. Tenant relationships. Rent collection. Capital improvements. If you're a property manager, Yardi is your daily work tool.

But Yardi operates at the property level. It doesn't know about the fund above it. Doesn't do fund-level waterfall calculations. Doesn't understand investor economics or side letters. Doesn't calculate carry.

So if you're a real estate fund manager using Yardi, you've got property-level financials. But to produce a fund-level capital account statement for an investor, you still need to pull data out of Yardi, reconcile it with fund-level admin data, and apply your fund-level calculations. In Excel. The property manager uses Yardi. The fund finance person uses Excel. And the two systems don't talk to each other.

One more dimension — joint ventures. Real estate funds often co-invest with operating companies. Maybe you're a twenty-five percent owner and they're seventy-five percent. Each JV has its own economics — different hurdle rates, different waterfall priorities, different management fees. Maybe the sponsor gets a promote on top of base carry.

You track your portion of the JV's performance and roll it up into fund-level reporting. But the JV partner might use different accounting systems, different valuation methods, different reporting periods. You get their spreadsheet. You validate it against your records. You reconcile differences. You make adjustments. You incorporate into your fund's performance. More Excel. And you're trusting that their Excel is correct even though you can't fully see into it.

So here's the summary. A real estate fund manager is dealing with property-level complexity from Yardi, fund-level complexity from the admin, multi-currency and hedge tracking, credit facility allocation with different interest rates, reclassification workflows, levered versus unlevered returns calculated in parallel, appraisal-based valuations, SOI entity structures with consolidation logic, and JV partner data that doesn't necessarily match yours.

All flowing through three separate systems at minimum — Yardi, the admin, and Excel — with no integration between them. Every LP report, every quarterly close, every new deal, every refinance, every property sale requires someone to manually orchestrate all this data.

LP Reporting and What Investors Need

Capital Accounts, Performance Metrics, and the Reporting Gap

We've walked through the entire ecosystem — administrators who maintain the books, the technology stack that's supposed to hold it all together, and the specific complexities of real estate. All of that infrastructure exists for one reason: to tell limited partners the truth about their investment.

Let's look at what that communication actually looks like. And why it's so hard to get right.

Start with a standard-setting body. The Institutional Limited Partners Association — ILPA — sets reporting standards for what GPs should tell their LPs. These standards exist because LPs got tired of receiving inconsistent reporting from different managers. One fund calculated returns one way. Another fund did it differently. Comparing performance across investments was nearly impossible. ILPA stepped in and said — here's what we expect to see. Most professional GPs now follow ILPA standards, though many add custom reporting on top.

The centerpiece of LP reporting is the Capital Account Statement. This is the document every LP sees every quarter. It's their account statement — like a bank statement, but for an illiquid investment fund. It shows beginning balance. Contributions during the period. Distributions received. Allocation of income or loss. Ending balance.

That ending balance is crucial. It's the value of the investment, according to the GP. It's what the LP theoretically owns. And it has to be calculated at the individual LP level — with that specific investor's economics baked in. If the fund has a hundred investors, you need a hundred capital account statements. Each one reflecting that investor's fee structure, preferred return, side letter overrides.

When an LP looks at their performance, they're usually focused on four key numbers.

First — IRR. Internal Rate of Return. A time-weighted return that accounts for when cash flows occur. If you invest a hundred thousand today and get back two hundred thousand in five years, the IRR tells you the annualized return per year. IRR is usually split into Gross IRR — before fees and carry — and Net IRR — after the GP has taken their cut. Most LPs care about Net because that's what they keep.

Second — MOIC. Multiple On Invested Capital. Simpler than IRR. Total value divided by total money invested. Invest a hundred thousand, get back three hundred thousand — your MOIC is 3x. Doesn't account for timing — you could get that return in two years or ten and the MOIC is the same. But it's easy to understand at a glance.

Third — DPI. Distributions to Paid-In capital. How much actual cash the LP has received relative to how much they invested. Invested a hundred thousand, received seventy thousand back — DPI is 0.7. DPI only counts real cash. Doesn't count unrealized gains.

Fourth — TVPI. Total Value to Paid-In. The complete picture. DPI plus the unrealized value of the remaining investment. Invested a hundred thousand, received seventy thousand in distributions, remaining investment worth fifty thousand — TVPI is 1.2. Twenty percent total return, but only seventy percent realized in cash.

GPs calculate these for every fund, every LP, every period. Fund-level and LP-level. Because the same fund can show different returns to different investors depending on their specific economics.

Now here's where complexity multiplies. Many sophisticated LPs aren't in just one fund. They're in multiple. Consider a large pension fund that invested in Fund I, Fund II, and Fund III from the same manager. They want blended performance across all three. Combined IRR. Blended multiple. Total capital deployed. Total received back. Overall relationship performance.

Answering this — blended performance across multiple funds — requires aggregating investor-level data across funds. You need the LP's position in each. Their IRR in each. Weighted by commitment size. Aggregated distributions and remaining value. Then calculate blended metrics.

The data lives in multiple places. Different fund instances. Maybe different portals. The calculation requires pulling from multiple systems, manually checking, building a model. That model lives in Excel. It won't auto-update. Next quarter the LP asks again. You rebuild.

For each question — you need data. But the data isn't in one place. Capital account calculations are in the GP's shadow books — the Excel model. Fund-level performance is in the GL or a reporting tool. LP-specific economics are somewhere else. You spend your time extracting, checking, modeling, producing one-off analyses.

And here's a key frustration. You don't have direct access to the calculation models. You can't open the waterfall tool and adjust assumptions. If a partner asks "what if we extended the fund by two years?" — you go to finance, ask them to run it, wait, and come back. Not instantaneous. Not collaborative. A back-and-forth that takes days.

Each audience needs different slices of the same data. Each needs different calculations or presentation formats. Typically each is getting their own separate Excel model or report. If the deal team's analysis uses slightly different assumptions than the CFO's — inconsistency. That's a real risk.

The Glossary Bridge

Twenty-Five Terms You Need to Know Cold

This is the quick-reference chapter. Twenty-five terms that come up in conversations with prospects, on Gong calls, in product demos, and in internal discussions. Some are industry terms. Some are Maybern-specific. All of them are terms where — if you hesitate or use the wrong definition — the person across from you notices.

I'm going to move fast. This chapter is designed to be replayed.

Let's start with Maybern-specific terms. These are words that mean something particular inside the product.

Fund family. The top-level organizational container in Maybern. Includes the main fund, GP entity, parallel funds, feeders, AIVs, blockers, co-invest vehicles. Everything in Maybern is configured at the fund family level. When someone says "set up a fund" — they mean create a fund family.

Investor class. A grouping of investors with the same economic terms — same fee rate, same waterfall definition, same allocation rules. This is how Maybern handles side letters at scale. Instead of forty individual overrides, you group investors into classes. Standard class, preferred class, co-invest class. Each class has its own configuration.

MXL. Maybern's expression language. The configurable formula system that drives waterfall tiers, fee basis calculations, performance metrics, and custom reports. Not a programming language — a configuration language. When someone says "that requires MXL" — they mean the logic is too complex for the basic UI and needs to be expressed in MXL formulas.

Capital event. A workflow container in Maybern that holds a capital call, a distribution, or both. When both happen simultaneously, it's called a net event. Capital events are the unit of work — everything related to a capital movement lives inside one event.

Transaction code. The fundamental building block. Every financial event in Maybern — contribution, distribution, fee payment, waterfall allocation — is categorized by a transaction code. Fee basis is built from transaction codes. Performance metrics are defined by transaction codes. Waterfall tiers reference transaction codes.

Waterfall definition. The configured waterfall logic for a fund. Contains the default configuration that applies to all investors unless overridden at the investor class level. Basic waterfalls can be configured in the UI. Complex and hybrid waterfalls require MXL.

Hypothetical waterfall. A waterfall calculation run for reporting purposes — not for actual distribution. Run at quarter-end to calculate unrealized carry accruals and produce capital account statements. Maybern supports both "actual" hypothetical waterfalls for finalized quarter-end reporting and "estimated" hypothetical waterfalls for mid-quarter projections.

Fee basis. The amount on which management fees are calculated. In Maybern, there are no pre-built "committed capital" or "invested capital" options. You build your fee basis by selecting which transaction codes feed into it. Fully configurable.

Allocation rule. Determines how capital events are distributed across entities. Pro-rata by ownership percentage is most common, but allocation rules are configurable. Not hardcoded.

Now — industry terms that you need to define correctly.

AIV — Alternative Investment Vehicle. A separate vehicle in a fund family used for specific investments where certain investors opt out, or for organizational purposes. Common when some LPs can't participate in a particular deal for legal or regulatory reasons.

Blocker. A taxable corporation — a C-Corp — created to block US taxable income for foreign investors or UBTI for tax-exempt investors. The blocker sits between the LP and the fund. The LP invests through the blocker to minimize tax exposure.

Feeder. A vehicle that aggregates multiple investors into a single entity to invest in the main fund. Common in structures where you need onshore and offshore feeders for different investor types. The feeder invests in the main fund as a single LP.

Parallel fund. An investment fund that operates alongside the main fund, sharing the same investment strategy but targeted at different investor types or jurisdictions. Parallel funds invest alongside the main fund in each deal.

PCAP — Partners' Capital Account Statement. The quarterly report each LP receives showing their beginning balance, contributions, distributions, income or loss allocation, and ending balance. This is the single most important document for LP communication. In Maybern, PCAPs are produced through hypothetical waterfall calculations.

NAV — Net Asset Value. Assets minus liabilities. Represents the equity value of the fund. An investor's NAV is their share of the fund's equity net of carried interest — it represents what they'd receive if the fund liquidated everything at fair value, paid liabilities, and ran the waterfall. Calculated quarterly.

Equalization. The process of truing up capital activity between new investors and existing investors after subsequent closings. The goal is to treat every investor as if they'd been in the fund since inception. Involves catch-up payments and equalization interest.

Fee vocabulary. There are four different fee modifications and people confuse them constantly.

Fee break. A permanent reduction in the management fee rate below the base LPA rate. Negotiated in a side letter. Lasts the life of the fund. LP pays one and a half percent instead of two percent — permanently.

Fee holiday. A temporary period where fees are reduced or eliminated. Maybe zero fees for the first two quarters, then regular fees kick in. Time-limited. The rate goes back to normal after the holiday ends.

Fee waiver. A mechanism where LPs fund a portion of the GP's capital contributions in return for a corresponding offset to their management fees. This is different from a break or a holiday — it's a cashless contribution arrangement.

Fee offset. A reduction in management fees caused by the fund bearing expenses subject to offsets — like excess organizational costs or placement fees — or by the GP receiving income directly from portfolio companies. Transaction fees, monitoring fees, exit fees — each might offset management fees at different percentages.

These four things are different. The glossary defines them differently. The LPA treats them differently. Maybern tracks them separately. If you use them interchangeably on a call with a fund controller, they'll notice.

A few more terms worth locking in.

Clawback. The GP's obligation to return excess carry at the end of the fund. If the GP received carry on early profitable exits but the overall fund underperforms — the GP returns the excess. Some carry is non-clawbackable, meaning it can't be returned even in a clawback scenario.

DPI — Distributions to Paid-In. How much cash an LP has received relative to what they invested. DPI of 0.7 means they've received seventy cents on every dollar invested. Only counts actual cash. Doesn't count unrealized gains.

TVPI — Total Value to Paid-In. The complete picture. Distributions received plus unrealized value, divided by capital invested. TVPI of 1.2 means twenty percent total return — but some of it might still be on paper.

Continuation fund. A new vehicle created to acquire assets from an existing fund nearing the end of its term. Used when the GP and some LPs want to keep holding the portfolio. Other LPs who prefer liquidity can exit through the continuation transaction.

Phantom income. Allocations of taxable income without associated cash distributions. The LP owes taxes on gains the fund hasn't actually distributed. This is why tax distributions exist — to give LPs cash to cover tax obligations on income they haven't received.
